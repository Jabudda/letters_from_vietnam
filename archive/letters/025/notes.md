## Open questions for the family

1. **The phone call and the date.** "Today is the day after I heard your wonderful voices." About 12:30 at night the guys "that came from Ben Het" were woken and patched through a MARS station in Kontum to one in the States, then by telephone to home, for about three minutes. With "6/21" on the envelope and "50 some days" to go, this is very likely **21 June 1969**, the call being on 20 June (interpretation). Letter 005 (19 or 20 June 1969) has him just in from Ben Het to Dak To. Does anyone remember that call? Who answered, Grandpa and Grandma?
2. **"At first I thought Dad was Mick."** Grandpa's and Mike's voices sounded alike on the radio?
3. **The bill.** "The army pays for the call up to the nearest MAR's station near home. Which probably is Chicago. So send me the bill." Did a bill come?
4. **"Unable to write for a few days."** He was worried they thought "something happened to me". This fits the days around the siege of Ben Het (Letter 005).
5. **The hunting dog.** "I sure hope to see a nice little hunting dog when I get home Dad. Let me pay for half too." In Letter 013 (6 August) Grandpa has a new pup and Dad again offers "to pay half". Andy has said all of Grandpa's dogs were named Fritz (German Shorthairs). Was the new pup another Fritz? Andy (2026-11-10): "Dad, like Grandpa were never creative with the dog names. I think Grandpa had 3 dogs named Fritz."
6. ~~**Fritz & Snoopy.**~~ "It's going to be hard to find a dog to replace Fritz & Snoopy." **Answered (Andy, 2026-11-10):** "Dad's Vietnam dog was Snoopy, but he may have been KIA." Dad's writing looks like "Snappy"; the family's reading is used. In Letter 027 (3 or 4 June 1969) Snoopy was alive and muddy; here, on about 21 June, Dad speaks of replacing him. That fits Snoopy having been lost between those dates, perhaps around Ben Het (**interpretation**; the letter does not say). Does anyone know what happened to Snoopy?
7. **The cycle.** "I also cancelled the deal on that cycle. Happy now. You all were right all along." This is very likely the Harley 1200 he asked about in Letter 009 (5 May). Andy has said Dad never bought the Harley; this letter says why, in Dad's words.
8. **Back to Ben Het.** "We have to get some Ammo ready to be air lifted to Ben Het to our Battery. I guess we'll be going back there." Did he go back? (Letter 018, 9 July, says the "operation in the Dak To - Ben Het area is over with".)

## Historical notes

- **Verified fact (from the letter):** The call was arranged for the men who "came from Ben Het", from "a regular field phone in our bunker", through a MARS station in Kontum to one in the United States, then home. It lasted about three minutes, and he had "trouble with saying 'over'". They could not say where they were or what they were doing.
- **Historical context:** The Military Affiliate Radio System (MARS) linked servicemen in Vietnam to their families by "phone patch": a short-wave radio contact with an amateur or military station in the United States, connected to the telephone network there. Each party had to say "over" when finished speaking. In 1969 alone, Air Force MARS stations in Vietnam and Thailand completed some 210,000 phone patches. *Sources:* [Air & Space Forces Magazine, "Our Affiliates From MARS" (Larry R. Morrison, April 1989)](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0489mars/); [Hotel 25 (Vietnam veterans' site), "MARS – Military Auxiliary Radio System"](https://hotel25vv.com/?p=10655).
- **Interpretation:** Calls were usually collect from the nearest U.S. station, which fits his "send me the bill" for the leg from "probably Chicago".
- **Historical context:** Ben Het was surrounded by North Vietnamese regiments from mid-June 1969 until early July (see Letter 005 notes and sources). This letter says only that he and others had come from Ben Het and that ammunition was to be airlifted to "our Battery" there. Nothing here places him in any particular engagement.
- **Unknown:** Where "our bunker" was (very likely the Dak To area, per Letter 005; interpretation).

### For younger readers: how a call from Vietnam worked in 1969

There were no cell phones, no internet and no video calls. Ordinary home phones were wired into the wall and dialled with a rotary dial, and a call across an ocean was rare and costly. A soldier in Vietnam usually called home through **MARS**, the Military Affiliate Radio System. His voice went by short-wave radio from a station in Vietnam (here, in Kontum) to a ham-radio or military station in the United States; an operator there plugged the radio into the ordinary telephone line and placed a collect call to the family. Because the radio could carry only one voice at a time, each person had to say **"over"** when finished speaking, like a walkie-talkie, and there was a delay before the other side answered. Calls were short, often about five minutes. In 1969 alone, Air Force MARS stations in Vietnam and Thailand completed about 210,000 of these "phone patches". *Sources:* [Air & Space Forces Magazine, "Our Affiliates From MARS" (Larry R. Morrison, April 1989)](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0489mars/); [Cherries (John Podlaski), "Calling Home During the Vietnam War"](https://cherrieswriter.com/2017/11/10/calling-home-during-the-vietnam-war/); [Hotel 25, "MARS – Military Auxiliary Radio System"](https://hotel25vv.com/?p=10655).

### News reports at home, June–July 1969

The family remembers that Grandma and Grandpa feared Dad had been killed because of what they heard in the news, and that one report said the entire company had been "wiped out". This is what has been found so far:

- **Historical context:** The siege of Ben Het was front-page news in the United States in late June 1969. Newspapers including the *Washington Post*, the *New York Times*, *Stars and Stripes* and the Lancaster, Pennsylvania papers carried daily reports from about 23 June to 11 July; one 1969 headline, quoted on a veterans' site, read "B52s SATURATE BEN HET JUNGLES" (*Stars and Stripes*, 26 June 1969), and another reported "BEN HET REINFORCEMENTS STALK REDS" (27 June), with an estimated 5,000 enemy shells since 6 May. *Sources:* [6th Battalion, 14th Artillery veterans, "Siege of Ben Het" (clippings kept by LTC Kenneth Bailey's family)](https://614arty.org/pages/ben_hetpg.html); [1st Battalion, 92nd Artillery Association, "Ben Het / Dak To, May–July 1969"](https://www.bravecannons.org/hist_benhet.html).
- **Historical context:** CBS Radio reported the end of the siege in early July 1969, describing "eight weeks of non-stop shelling". *Source:* [Lowell Thomas CBS Radio script, July 1969 (Marist College archives, PDF)](https://exhibits.archives.marist.edu/files/original/dcf42ee449c227cce0d93e7a7341cea0225c7c02.pdf).
- **Historical context:** Dad's own battalion was named in the record of the siege: A Battery, 3rd Battalion, 6th Artillery had a platoon of self-propelled 105 mm howitzers at Ben Het (Fire Support Base 12). *Sources:* LTC Kenneth Bailey's letter on the 614arty.org page above; [1/92nd Artillery, Operational Report for the period ending 31 July 1969](https://www.bravecannons.org/History/ORLL/j_may69-jul69.html).
- **Historical context:** Artillery units around Dak To and Ben Het took heavy losses in May and June 1969; one artillery veteran recalls his battery's casualty rate in those two months as about 60 per cent. *Source:* [Siege of Dak To, comments by veterans (charliecompany.org)](https://charliecompany.org/2013/01/10/siege-of-dak-to/).
- **Historical context:** A U.S. Air Force history written during the war, *The Siege of Ben Het* (Project CHECO, 1 October 1969, by Ernie S. Montagliani), dates the heavy shelling from the first week of May 1969. It records that the enemy lifted the siege and withdrew on 29 June and that advisers declared it over on 2 July. It notes that *Newsweek* (7 July) and *Time* (11 July) covered the siege, that the press reported the road open while a convoy was still fighting its way in, and it warns that headlines can "inflate an event far beyond its real significance". Ben Het held; the report does not describe any American unit being wiped out. *Source:* [Project CHECO, *The Siege of Ben Het* (DTIC ADA487237, PDF)](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA487237.pdf); also posted on [Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/81658082/10-1-1969-the-Siege-of-Ben-Het) (found by Andy).
- **Historical context:** At Dak To, about eight miles east of Ben Het, engineers of the 299th Engineer Battalion and the artillery supporting them were shelled almost daily from 9 May into July 1969. The four companies defending Dak To had about 45 per cent casualties; artillerymen there lost 6 killed and 25 wounded, including 4 killed and 11 wounded by rockets on 23 May. These were not Dad's battery. *Source:* [Marc Leepson, "The Defenders of Dak To", *The VVA Veteran*, January/February 2012](https://vvaveteran.org/32-1/32-1_dakto69.html) (found by Andy).
- **Historical context (the closest match found so far):** An Associated Press story filed from Ben Het, printed on the front page of the Lancaster, Pennsylvania *Daily Intelligencer Journal* on Friday 27 June 1969 under the headline "Men At Ben Het Feel Victimized", was about **A Battery, 3rd Battalion, 6th Artillery**, Dad's battalion, dug in on "North Hill" at Ben Het with six self-propelled 105 mm guns. It reported: "Casualties in the battery have passed the 100 mark" in the previous two weeks, leaving the battery at times at only half strength. It described sappers blowing up one gun and killing one of its crew, men without fresh water, and the wounded waiting for medevac helicopters; the battery commander, Capt. John Horalek of Fort Collins, Colorado, said the battery had been hit hard. It compared the men's situation with the worst days of Khe Sanh and Con Thien. AP stories went to newspapers across the country. *Source:* clipping reproduced on the [6th Battalion, 14th Artillery veterans' "Siege of Ben Het" page](https://614arty.org/pages/ben_hetpg.html) (image `ben_het_news_6_27di.jpg`), read in full 2026-11-10.
- **Historical context:** A day earlier (26 June 1969) another AP story, printed in the *Washington Post*, said more than 100 U.S. artillerymen and Green Berets had been killed or wounded in the shelling of Ben Het since 1 June, and that a U.S. officer thought the enemy could probably take the camp if willing to pay the price. A UPI story in *Stars and Stripes* (4 July 1969) put U.S. losses for the whole siege at at least 14 killed and 132 wounded. On 23 June a dispatch reported recoilless-rifle fire on "the Benhet artillery position" and a ground probe, with one American killed and six wounded. *Source:* clippings on the same 614arty.org page (`wp_ben_het_news_6_26.jpg`, `ben_het_news_7_4ss.jpg`, `ben_het_news_6_23.jpg`; Andy also kept a copy of the 23 June clipping).
- **Historical context:** *Time* magazine (issue of 11 July 1969, "The Lesson of Ben Het") said the press had seized on Ben Het and that some stories warned the outpost might be overrun. *Source:* [Time](https://time.com/archive/6637066/world-the-lesson-of-ben-het) (copy saved by Andy).
- **Interpretation:** Dad's letters connect him to A Battery: on 7 June 1969 he wrote that he was going to Ben Het to help A Battery, which had 22 men in hospital (Letter 026), and on about 21 June he wrote of airlifting ammunition "to Ben Het to our Battery" (this letter). A newspaper or broadcast saying that his battalion's battery at Ben Het had passed 100 casualties and was at half strength, in the same week a camp "might be overrun", is the kind of report that could be remembered as the whole company being "wiped out". This is the closest match found; the words "wiped out" themselves have not been found.
- **Unknown:** A news report using the words "wiped out" has **not been found yet**. Andy's 22 clippings from the 614arty.org page and the reports above do not use them. Whether the AP story about A Battery ran in the Milwaukee papers, and what Grandma and Grandpa saw or heard, is not known. It may have been a local Milwaukee newspaper, radio or television report, or the CBS Evening News (Dad had written in Letter 005, "If you watch the CBS news you may get lucky and see me"). Next places to look: *Newsweek* (7 July 1969) and *Time* (11 July 1969), the *Milwaukee Journal* and *Milwaukee Sentinel* for about 20–30 June 1969, and CBS, NBC and ABC evening news abstracts for June 1969 in the [Vanderbilt Television News Archive](https://tvnews.library.vanderbilt.edu/).
- **Interpretation:** Dad's words fit the family's memory: "At first I was a bit worried cause I thought you may have thought something happened to me. But I'm glad we got that straight. Especially after we were unable to write for a few days a phone call is just what was needed."
