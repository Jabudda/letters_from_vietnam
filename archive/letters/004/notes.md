## Historical notes

- **Verified fact (from the envelope):** His return address is "T. Jafuta, HHB 3/6 ARTY, APO SAN FRAN. 96318". "Free" is written in place of a stamp.
- **Historical context:** The 3rd Battalion, 6th Artillery was a 105 mm self-propelled howitzer battalion made up of a headquarters and headquarters battery (HHB), a service battery and three howitzer batteries; its headquarters used APO 96318. *Source:* [3rd Battalion, 6th Artillery, Operational Report, 4 November 1967](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD0387394.pdf) (Defense Technical Information Center, AD0387394).
- **Historical context:** The battalion arrived at Pleiku in June 1966 and was based at Camp Saint Barbara, later called Artillery Hill, outside Pleiku, as part of the 52nd Artillery Group, I Field Force Vietnam Artillery. *Sources:* [Mike's Research, "M108 and M109 Self-Propelled Howitzers in Vietnam"](https://mikesresearch.com/2025/12/30/m108-and-m109-self-propelled-howitzers-in-vietnam); [1/92nd Field Artillery Association, "The Organization of the Field Artillery in Vietnam"](https://www.bravecannons.org/the_gun/unit_org.html) and [Operational Report, Nov 1968 – Jan 1969](https://www.bravecannons.org/History/ORLL/h_nov_jan_69.html).
- **Verified fact (family):** Tom served in the headquarters battery (HHB) of the 3rd Battalion, 6th Artillery, as his return address shows. The family confirmed this (Andy, 2026-11-10). It fits Letter 001 ("back in Pleiku with my unit") and Letter 002 ("when the guns go off").
- **Verified fact (from the letter):** He fixed "a bunker line" with "some short in it", and got a shock when "all the battery started calling in" while he was touching two lines.
- **Interpretation:** This reads like work on field telephone wires between bunkers: ringing a field telephone sends a pulse of current down the line, enough to give a shock but, as he says, not "enough to hurt". The letter does not name his job.
- **Verified fact (from the letter):** He writes that "the unit next to us got the hell knocked out of them" that day. He does not name the unit or say what happened, and this note does not link it to any particular event.
- **Interpretation:** His "Jan 15th… the 14th back home" reflects the time difference: Vietnam's clock was many hours ahead of Wisconsin's (roughly 14 hours in winter), so for most of the day it was still the day before at home.
- **Verified fact (family):** Tom turned 21 in early January 1969 (family records), so "ol' (all 21 yrs.)" fits January 1969.
- **Verified fact (VA record):** His father, Peter F. Jafuta, served in the US Army in World War II as a Technician Fourth Grade. *Source:* [US Department of Veterans Affairs, Veterans Legacy Memorial, Peter F Jafuta](https://www.vlm.cem.va.gov/PETERFJAFUTA/35385A).
- **Historical context:** When Tom wrote this letter there was no medical diagnosis for lasting war trauma, but earlier generations did have names for it. *Source:* [VA National Center for PTSD, "History of PTSD in Veterans: Civil War to DSM-5"](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/understand/what/history_ptsd.asp). Examples:
    - **"Nostalgia"** (1700s): a homesick, low, sleepless state seen in soldiers.
    - **"Soldier's heart"** or **"irritable heart"** (US Civil War): seen as a physical ailment, with a racing pulse and trouble breathing.
    - **"Shell shock"** (World War I): first blamed on hidden damage from exploding shells, though men far from any explosion had it too. "War neurosis" was also used.
    - **"Battle fatigue"**, or combat stress reaction (World War II, Grandpa's war).
- **Historical context:** The American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual listed "gross stress reaction" from 1952, but dropped it in 1968, the year before this letter. Between 1968 and 1980 American psychiatrists had no official diagnosis for trauma. "Post-traumatic stress disorder" was added in 1980 (DSM-III), based on research with returning Vietnam veterans, Holocaust survivors, sexual trauma victims and others. Vietnam veterans pushed hard for it to be recognized. *Sources:* [VA National Center for PTSD](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/understand/what/history_ptsd.asp); [BrainFacts.org (Society for Neuroscience), "The Changing Face of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder", 2019](https://www.brainfacts.org/diseases-and-disorders/mental-health/2019/the-changing-face-of-posttraumatic-stress-disorder-062019).
- **Unknown:** The letter does not say what Tom meant by "Dad knows what I mean". The family's reading is given under Family notes.

## Family notes for the chapter

- **"Dad's birthday (I think)"** (page 1), in Andy's words: "Dad would usually get the birthday dates wrong, even me, his own son. The only reason he knew my birthday was in November and approximate date is because it aligned with deer hunting season." (Andy, 2026-11-10)
- **"Dad knows what I mean"** (page 2), Andy's interpretation: "I speculate the Dad knows what I mean is a reference to the impact of war. The Vietnam veterans were the PTSD pioneers, prior to Vietnam it did not have a name." His own father had served in the Army in World War II (see Historical notes). (Andy, 2026-11-10)
    - *Editor's note:* Earlier wars did have names for it, such as "shell shock" in World War I and "battle fatigue" in World War II, but it was not a medical diagnosis. "PTSD" was named in 1980, and Vietnam veterans were central to that. See Historical notes for examples and sources.
- Andy added: "My birth date is November 18. Dad usually thought my birthday was on November 17 as that is when I was supposed to be born and the year I was born is the only year he missed deer hunting season." (Andy, 2026-11-10)
